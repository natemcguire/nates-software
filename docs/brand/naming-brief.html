Purpose: Align naming work around a distinctive, defensible masterbrand for a consumer marketplace where software can be owned, changed, and recombined.

How to use this brief: Approve it before generating candidates; use it to create broad naming territories, screen every presented candidate, and resolve feedback against agreed criteria rather than personal taste.

Naming Brand Brief

1. The assignment

Create the masterbrand name for a consumer-facing software marketplace, creator economy, and underlying git platform. The name must lead with an inviting consumer promise while retaining enough authority for serious technical infrastructure.

The brand must stretch across:

  • a daily discovery marketplace with launches, rankings, votes, badges, and maker profiles;
  • owned copies of real software that people can customize with AI and exchange features among, with real git and safe undo underneath;
  • free, one-time, and subscription commerce for apps and features, with revenue shared through the ancestry chain; and
  • a high-speed, reliability-led git engine and an advanced mode for developers.

This is a platform masterbrand, not a feature name. It should still make sense if the product expands beyond its first interface, current AI tools, or today's app formats.

Assumed launch scope: U.S., English-first, with global internet visibility. Confirm geography before finalist clearance.

2. Strategic foundation

Positioning

For people who want software to adapt to them, this is a marketplace and ownership platform where every app becomes a modifiable copy. Unlike app stores, launch directories, AI prototyping tools, or developer-only code hosts, it moves features among related apps, keeps changes reversible on real git, and shares value with creators whose work lives on.

Brand promise

Software becomes something people can truly make their own—without needing to understand source control.

Payoff: discover, acquire, customize, combine, undo, publish, and earn—creating agency without anxiety and creative play backed by real ownership.

Proof and differentiation

  1. Ownership: the user receives a modifiable copy rather than a fixed, opaque download.

  2. Living lineage: features can move downstream, sideways, or upstream among related copies.

  3. Safe AI modification: AI performs semantic merges; real git and reliable undo sit underneath.

  4. Compounding creator value: revenue and attribution can continue through descendant work.

  5. Infrastructure credibility: the merge queue is designed for agent-speed volume, with measurable speed and reliability positioned against incumbent code hosts.

  6. Maker-club lineage: the platform is homebrew-adjacent — heir to the Homebrew Computer Club, ROM-hacking, and mod-scene tradition of hobbyists tinkering, swapping builds, and showing off. Its twist is that AI admits non-developers as full members of that club for the first time.

The name should open a story around one or two of these ideas, not attempt to explain all six.

Positioning-route requirement

The editorial model is intentionally unresolved. A viable name must support all three futures:

  • an expert, trusted-reviewer voice;
  • a community-ranked marketplace; or
  • a hybrid community platform with a strong editorial layer.

A candidate may have a point of view, but cannot make any route feel contradictory.

3. Audience

Primary: software owners, not git users

Non-technical consumers aged roughly 25–45 who use apps, marketplaces, and AI assistants but do not identify as developers. They want software to fit their lives and value plain language, reversibility, proof, and control. The name cannot require prior knowledge of git mechanics (branches, commits, merge queues); concepts like "fork" or "mod" are acceptable if learnable in one sentence of product copy ("you get a copy you can change").

Secondary: AI-native makers and developers

Builders who care about source access, portability, merge speed, lineage, monetization, and raw git interoperability. The name must feel credible, not like a toy, shallow directory, or no-code cul-de-sac.

Shared audience truth

Both groups want leverage, independence, and belonging to a maker community. This is a club of people who tinker, swap builds, and show their work — and the "new makers" are non-developers joining that tradition for the first time. Whether the interface hides or exposes the machinery, the system must feel powerful, trustworthy, participatory, and fun.

4. Personality and tone

The visual world combines Windows 95-style chrome played straight, benchmark-minded density, and launch-day energy. The name should fit without becoming a 1990s costume.

5. What we are not

  • Not an incumbent code-host clone, enterprise DevOps suite, launch directory, or one-day popularity contest.
  • Not a conventional app store for fixed binaries, or a prompt-to-prototype/closed no-code builder.
  • Not piracy, creator extraction, crypto, multi-level marketing, or a passive-income scheme.
  • Not a plugin/theme bazaar; complete apps and transferable features are first-class goods.
  • Not a nostalgia museum, editorial-only publication, or crowd-only platform.

7. Naming directions and constructs

Develop territories before words. Relevant fields are personal agency, living lineage, safe transformation, exchange and reciprocity, playful workmanship, measured performance, and hobbyist-club culture (the garage, workshop, swap-meet, and homebrew-computing tradition of makers trading and improving each other's builds). These are stories, not literal word lists.

Note on the homebrew territory: the Homebrew package manager and homebrew game/ROM scenes already occupy the literal word; treat "homebrew" as a cultural reference point for territory development, not as name material.

Approach spectrum

Approach Role in exploration Target share of initial candidate pool
Descriptive / functional A clarity control; likely weakest for differentiation and legal strength 10%
Suggestive / experiential Hints at the benefit or experience without explaining mechanics 30%
Evocative / metaphorical Expresses the positioning through a richer, ownable association 35%
Invented / abstract Maximizes distinctiveness if it remains warm, speakable, and meaningful in context 25%

Explore real words, unexpected compounds, short phrases, and coined forms. Do not default to a familiar tech word with a fashionable prefix or suffix.

Linguistic requirements

  • Prefer one word; two short words are acceptable. Favor 2–4 syllables and 4–14 letters. It should have one obvious U.S.-English pronunciation and pass a one-introduction hear-say-spell test.
  • Prefer ASCII letters. Do not depend on capitalization, punctuation, hyphens, numerals, diacritics, or a logo to disambiguate the name.
  • Work alone and with plain descriptors such as “app marketplace,” “creator platform,” “developer docs,” and “status”; allow natural possessive and adjectival use.
  • Stay distinct in a leaderboard, browser title bar, terminal, and domain, and sound credible from consumers, creators, operators, and reviewers.

Avoid

  • Literal dependence on “AI,” “git,” “merge,” “repo,” “commit,” “code,” “app,” or “store.” Note on “fork”: allowed if used to teach ownership as an intuitive consumer action/place (e.g. compounds like FORKYARD or Forklore) rather than developer plumbing or a code-host clone (e.g. ForkHub is still out). Mechanics may inform a territory, but cannot make the brand sterile, overly technical, or generic.
  • Category-copying “hub,” “lab,” “hunt,” “list,” or “forge” constructions; faux-classical SaaS coinages; gratuitous misspellings; compressed consonants; and trend suffixes.
  • Language that implies theft, cloning without attribution, danger, irreversibility, lock-in, financial speculation, or guaranteed earnings.
  • A childish-game, enterprise-infrastructure, or publication register that excludes other uses; or terms too common for search, voice, domains, or protection.

8. Evaluation scorecard

Rate 1 (fails) to 5 (exceptional), then calculate rating / 5 × weight. Advance only candidates scoring 75+, with no score below 3 in strategic fit, audience fit, or distinctiveness, and no hard-screen failure.

Criterion Weight Evaluation question
Strategic fit 20 Does it create a credible story around ownership, living software, creator value, or trusted power?
Audience accessibility 15 Is it inviting to non-technical consumers without losing developer respect?
Competitive distinctiveness 15 Does it stand apart from code hosts, launch sites, app stores, and AI builders?
Memorability and verbal quality 10 Is it easy to recall, say, hear, and recommend?
Personality and tone 10 Is it fun, gutsy, direct, and dependable rather than gimmicky or corporate?
Stretch and route resilience 10 Can it carry the marketplace, community, editorial layer, creator economy, and infrastructure over time?
Linguistic and cultural usability 5 Is it clean in launch languages and free of serious unintended meanings?
Preliminary trademark feasibility 10 Is it distinctive and plausibly clear of confusingly similar marks for related goods/services?
Digital practicality 5 Is there a credible domain, handle, search, package, and marketplace path?

Do not ask which candidate people “like best.” In equal context, test recall, pronunciation, spelling, associations, category expectation, and negative meanings.

9. Practical screens and hard gates

Basic-check every presented candidate and fully screen every finalist. Record source, date, query, result, and risk; availability is only a snapshot.

Digital and marketplace screen

  • Check exact-match .com and .dev status, use, history, acquisition status, price, and typos. Exact .com is preferred; .dev is a technical/defensive option, not a cure for brand conflict. Reject a primary domain requiring a hyphen, confusing modifier, or hard-to-dictate spelling.
  • Check close variants across GitHub organizations/users/repositories, npm and relevant registries, Product Hunt, Apple App Store, Google Play, Microsoft Store, and the web.
  • Check search ambiguity, voice/email dictation, typos, and practical handles on X, Bluesky, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, LinkedIn, and GitHub. Exact handles are preferred, not mandatory.

Trademark screen

  • Run a USPTO word-mark knockout for exact, partial, phonetic, spelling, meaning, and commercial-impression similarities. At minimum, sanity-check Class 9 for downloadable software/apps and Class 42 for SaaS/PaaS, development, hosting, and related services.
  • Do not restrict searches to those classes; related goods/services can conflict across classes. Ask counsel whether Class 35 marketplace/advertising and Class 36 payment/revenue-distribution services belong in scope.
  • Search live federal marks, relevant dead marks still in use, state/business records, product listings, domains, and web/common-law use. Eliminate obvious high-risk conflicts before presentation. Naming-team searches are triage; U.S.-licensed trademark counsel must clear the finalist.

Linguistic and international screen

  • Review finalists in U.S. English and U.S. Spanish with at least two target-audience native speakers. Disaster-check French, German, Brazilian Portuguese, Japanese, and Mandarin Chinese for sound-alikes, transliteration, slang, negative meaning, pronunciation, and confusing brands; expand to each planned market.
  • Reject serious vulgar, discriminatory, unsafe, deceptive, or culturally charged meanings; document mild or audience-specific concerns.

Usage stress test

Place each finalist, in identical typography, into these neutral contexts:

  • “I found it on [NAME] and made my own version.”
  • “Publish your app on [NAME].”
  • “View the family, features, and creator history on [NAME].” / “[NAME] status.”
  • “Connect to [NAME] over SSH.”

A finalist must work in conversation, marketplace UI, editorial copy, technical docs, a terminal, and a support incident.

10. Requested process and deliverables

  1. Alignment: confirm launch markets, decision owner, legal risk tolerance, domain budget, and approve this brief.
  2. Territory map: present 5–7 territories with rationale and competitive separation; do not select candidates yet.
  3. Generation: create at least 250 raw candidates across the spectrum. The raw list may remain internal; report coverage and filters.
  4. Round 1: present 18–24 screened candidates with pronunciation, construct, rationale, score, and collision notes in equal typography.
  5. Round 2: present 8–12 refined and new candidates, with preliminary .com/.dev, handle, marketplace, linguistic, and USPTO Class 9/42 findings.
  6. Finalists: advance 3–5 to deeper common-law, international, domain-acquisition, and native-speaker review in identical usage contexts.
  7. Recommendation: deliver one recommended finalist and two viable alternates with scores, tradeoffs, pronunciation, story, screening log, and asset-lock plan.
  8. Clear and secure: after counsel clears the name, purchase domains and reserve handles. Filing, negotiation, visual identity, and taglines are separate scopes unless added.

Tie feedback to a criterion and evidence. Personal associations are inputs, not automatic vetoes. Appoint one client-side decision owner before Round 1.
